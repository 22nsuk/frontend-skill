# Web pages

Use this reference when designing how a public page explains an offering, establishes relevance, or leads to an action. Apply the portions that match the brief, including editorial, community, and informational sites that have no sales objective.

## Build a useful sequence

Start from the questions a visitor needs answered: what this is, whether it is relevant, what supports its claims, and what can happen next. Decide their order from the audience's familiarity and the material available. A product explanation, an event schedule, and a service directory need different structures.

Compose the opening with enough context to orient the visitor and an appropriate next step. Its height follows the material and device. A large image, compact introduction, article opening, or comparison table can each be appropriate. Do not reserve a full screen or introduce an oversized headline simply because the page has a beginning.

Use section boundaries to mark a change of question or subject. Remove repeated introductions and empty framing while retaining qualifications and useful detail. Short pages do not need a fixed inventory of sections, testimonials, pricing blocks, or repeated buttons.

## Make the subject visible

Choose media that helps visitors recognize the actual offering, process, place, or result. A software demonstration should show the activity being explained, with readable content at its intended display size. A static illustration of an interface must not suggest that its controls work.

Use supplied assets when they are suitable. Before adding new imagery, consider whether a clearer arrangement of existing material would serve better. Keep illustrative or proposed content distinguishable from a photograph of a real customer's premises, a real result, or a verified endorsement. Retain helpful captions, source information, and accessible alternatives.

Decide the visual character through coherent choices in composition, typography, and imagery. It can be energetic, formal, tactile, or understated as the subject requires. Do not manufacture a logo, effect, or decorative object merely to satisfy a uniqueness test.

## Make actions trustworthy

Place the next action where the visitor has enough context to understand it. Label its actual result: navigating, downloading, requesting contact, and completing a purchase are different commitments. Repetition is useful only where the reading path makes the action otherwise difficult to find.

Check destinations and distinguish a working integration from a demonstrator. Do not invent a successful submission, endorsement, availability claim, or business detail. When necessary information is missing, make the uncertainty apparent and complete the remaining authorized work. Request clarification when it changes a consequential action.

## Inspect the page as a visitor

Follow the intended reading route and its main links at the relevant display sizes. Check that actual assets, long headings, navigation, and overlays leave the key information and action reachable. Confirm the meaning of each prominent claim and action. Limit additional inspection to the changed material and concrete risks.
